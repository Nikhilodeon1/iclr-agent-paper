\begin{abstract}
Backdoor poisoning of language-model pretraining has been observed to succeed after a
near-constant number of poisoned documents, largely independent of the amount of clean
data, while packing more poison into each batch makes the attack \emph{less}
sample-efficient. Neither finding has a mechanistic explanation. We give closed-form accounts of both in an idealised scalar model. In a solvable model of online training, a trigger that never occurs in clean data ($q=0$) is learned from poison alone, so under SGD the threshold depends
on the poison count and not on clean-data size; when the trigger also appears in clean data at frequency $q$, a mean-field fixed point makes the threshold the number of competing clean uses $q\,n_{\text{clean}}$, which has the same scaling form as the dense-overlap regime of prior theory under a formal identification of parameters (we do not compare quantitatively with their predictions). A test fixed before running, on a transformer trained
from scratch, is consistent with this: the poison needed for a trigger that
also appears in clean data is of the order of the predicted count of competing clean uses (123, 346 and 914 examples against a predicted 100, 300 and 900 as training length triples), while a trigger absent from clean data needs 39 (interval 33--42) at the longest length and fewer than 25 at the two shorter ones. Under
Adam, which is how language models are trained, a second resource appears: because
trigger-specific parameters receive gradient only on poisoned steps, each poisoned
\emph{batch} moves them by a fixed amount regardless of how many poison examples it
contains, and batches closer together than the second-moment memory $1/(1-\beta_2)$
interfere. This zero-parameter prediction correlates with attack success
across the 11 schedule, length and placement conditions we ran (Spearman $0.67$,
$p=0.02$), though pooled over heterogeneous groups and so weak on its own, and direct measurements bear on it. One poisoned batch moves the trigger's embedding row by the same amount, to within 3\% of the closed form, whether it holds 1 or 64 poison examples under AdamW, and in proportion to the number of examples under SGD. At 20 seeds and a fixed $N=16$, splitting the poison over 16 batches rather than 1 takes AdamW from $5/20$ to $13/20$ runs succeeding (paired exact $p=0.008$) while SGD stays at floor ($1/20$). With 20 seeds, moving only the trigger's embedding row from AdamW to SGD removes most of the batch-count effect at $N=16$ ($15/20$ against $7/20$ successes at 16 batches, Fisher $p=0.025$). Two tests fixed in advance of the predicted interference between nearby poisoned batches, varying $\beta_2$, were not supported, the second with step size matched by construction and measurement, so we withdraw that prediction; and a learning-rate sweep did not follow the $1/\eta$ budget of Equation~\ref{eq:budget} as a global slope. Continuing pretraining of a pretrained model on natural
text, with trigger frequencies measured from the corpus rather than imposed, reproduces
the predicted ordering: the poison needed rises monotonically with corpus frequency and
a never-occurring trigger is cheapest to implant by $3.5$--$5\times$. Two sweeps specified in advance bear on scale and density: the poison threshold is flat from $0.8$M to $25$M parameters (log-log slope $-0.02$, interval $[-0.15,0.08]$) but more than five times higher at $0.11$M, and it grows with per-batch density as $k^{0.42}$ (interval $[0.23,0.55]$), between the batch-only prediction of $1$ and the example-only prediction of $0$. We state two limits plainly. The range is far below the $600$M--$13$B parameters of \citet{souly2025poisoning}; and count invariance holds only within a window
of training lengths, beyond which attack success decays in a way our account predicts in
direction but underestimates in size. The SGD arm of the optimizer comparison sits at
floor, so it bounds rather than establishes the contrast.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Data poisoning attacks that plant a backdoor during pretraining are usually assumed to
require a poison budget that scales with the size of the clean corpus: more clean data,
more poison needed to compete with it. \citet{souly2025poisoning} overturned this
assumption empirically. Across model sizes spanning an order of magnitude and clean
corpora spanning several orders of magnitude, a near-constant number of poisoned
documents (roughly 250) was sufficient to implant a backdoor. Two further findings
concern scheduling. At a fixed per-batch poison density, the frequency of poisoned
batches did not change how many poison samples were needed; but at a higher per-batch
density, \emph{more} poison samples were needed. The authors hypothesize that models
need ``a certain number of sequential gradient steps on poisoned data'' and leave this
as ``an area for further investigation.'' Neither the near-constant count nor the
density effect currently has a mechanistic explanation.

This near-constant-count regime is in apparent tension with an established line of
poisoning theory that treats the attack's success threshold as governed by the
\emph{fraction} of poisoned training examples, not their absolute count
\citep{linearpoisoning2025,glmbackdoor2026}, both derived via random-matrix and
statistical-physics techniques for high-dimensional linear and generalized linear
models. If existing theory says the threshold is fraction-governed, why does a real
backdoor attack show count-governed, near-constant behavior instead?

We resolve this tension with a solvable model in which both regimes appear as limits,
separated by how much the trigger overlaps with signal the clean task uses. When the
trigger never occurs in clean data, clean training exerts no pull on it while the
implanted margin is small, and under SGD the attack depends on the poison count alone.
When the trigger also occurs in clean data, the two compete and the threshold is set by the number of competing clean uses, which is the regime of prior fraction-based theory. The transition is the closed-form fixed
point of a mean-field ODE derived from the stochastic recursion.

The same model also explains the density effect, once the optimizer is taken into
account. Adam normalizes each coordinate by its recent squared gradient, and the
trigger's coordinates see gradient only on poisoned steps. Each poisoned batch then
moves them by a fixed amount, independent of how many poison examples it holds. This
makes the ``number of sequential gradient steps on poisoned data'' conjectured by
\citet{souly2025poisoning} precise: under Adam the attack counts poisoned batches,
discounted when they fall within the second-moment memory of one another.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A closed-form account of why the poison needed for a rare-trigger backdoor
    does not grow with clean-data size (Proposition~\ref{thm:count-regime}), and of how it becomes proportional to the number of competing clean uses as the trigger's clean frequency grows (Proposition~\ref{prop:fraction-regime}), unifying the count-based empirical
    findings with fraction-based poisoning theory.
    \item A closed-form result for an idealized Adam recurrence
    (Proposition~\ref{prop:adam-events}): for trigger-specific coordinates, the
    displacement caused by a poisoned batch is independent of how many poison examples
    that batch contains, and depends instead on the spacing between batches, with an explicit interference formula (which our two tests did not support). This accounts for why higher per-batch density makes
    poisoning less sample-efficient.
    \item Validation on a transformer trained from scratch, including a fixed before running
    test of the $q$ transition, and an account of where the theory's reach
    ends: count invariance holds only within a window of training lengths, and the
    decay beyond it is predicted in direction but not in magnitude.
    \item A controlled optimizer intervention (Section~\ref{sec:events}): on the same
    transformer, holding the total poison count fixed and varying how many batches it
    is split into raises attack success under AdamW but not under SGD. At 20 seeds AdamW goes from $5/20$ to $13/20$ successes at $N=16$ (paired exact $p=0.008$; the Wilson intervals just touch) against an SGD arm at floor ($1/20$), which bounds rather than establishes the contrast.
    \item Direct tests of the Adam mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:direct}): a measurement of the trigger embedding's displacement that matches the closed form (supported), two pre-specified $\beta_2$ tests of interference (neither supported; the second matches step size by construction and measurement, so we withdraw the prediction), and a 20-seed parameter-group test that supports the mechanism at $N=16$ ($15/20$ successes at 16 batches with the trigger row on AdamW against $7/20$ on SGD, $p=0.025$) but is uninformative at $N=32$, where the arms are near ceiling.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Empirical poisoning at pretraining scale.} \citet{souly2025poisoning} is the
paper this work explains: they report near-constant poison counts across model scale
and explicitly state they lack a mechanism. Backdoor attacks that tie a trigger to a target behaviour were introduced by
\citet{gu2017badnets} and \citet{chen2017targeted}, following earlier work on poisoning
convex learners \citep{biggio2012poisoning}, with clean-label variants
\citep{shafahi2018poison} and unified benchmarks \citep{schwarzschild2021toxic}. In
language and multimodal settings, poisoning has been demonstrated on pretrained weights
\citep{kurita2020weight}, NLP models \citep{wallace2021concealed}, contrastive
pretraining \citep{carlini2022contrastive}, web-scale corpora
\citep{carlini2023poisoning} and instruction tuning \citep{wan2023poisoning}. \citet{bowen2024scaling} report scaling trends for poisoning
susceptibility with model size, and \citet{zhang2024persistent} show that
pretraining-stage backdoors persist through subsequent training. These establish that
small poison budgets suffice and that the effect grows with scale; none characterises
the count-versus-fraction distinction or its dependence on trigger rarity. Our decay
observations are also related to work measuring forgetting of memorised training
examples~\citep{jagielski2023forgetting}.

\textbf{Theoretical analyses of backdoor success.} The closest theoretical treatment is
\citet{wang2024demystifying}, who bound a compromised model's clean and backdoor
accuracy for a constant trigger and identify determining factors for attack success.
Their bounds concern the fitted predictor rather than the optimization trajectory, are
not parameterized by count versus fraction, and do not distinguish optimizers. \citet{cina2024curves} study backdoor learning curves via influence
functions and show that effectiveness depends on the learner's hyperparameters, on the
\emph{fraction} of poisoned samples, and on trigger size. That hyperparameter dependence is the closest existing statement to our optimizer
result, but it is empirical, for convex learners, and parameterized by fraction; it does
not identify second-moment normalization as the mechanism. Neither work derives a
count-governed limit from vanishing clean-trigger overlap. Defences exploiting the geometry a backdoor leaves
behind \citep{tran2018spectral,steinhardt2017certified} are complementary: we speak to
which triggers are cheap to implant, not to how they are detected.

\textbf{Fraction-governed poisoning theory.} \citet{linearpoisoning2025} derive a
closed-form poisoning threshold for ridge regression via random matrix theory,
parameterizing the attack by the \emph{fraction} $\theta$ of one class relabeled along a
fixed direction. \citet{glmbackdoor2026} study trigger \emph{strength} at a fixed
poison ratio in a high-dimensional generalized linear model and find a non-monotonic
success curve as strength varies. Both consider trigger directions with non-negligible
overlap with the ambient signal, qualitatively analogous to our $q>0$ regime, and
neither addresses a count-governed limit, scheduling invariance, or dataset-size
invariance. Under a formal identification of parameters (Section~\ref{sec:prop2}) our threshold has the same scaling form as theirs; we have not compared our predictions with their random-matrix results quantitatively. The two also count different things: their poisoned fraction is taken over the whole training set, ours over occurrences of the trigger, so the match is formal rather than a unification.

\textbf{Theoretical tools.} Our derivation uses a mean-field/stochastic-approximation
reduction of a discrete SGD recursion to an autonomous ODE, in the spirit of classical
stochastic approximation theory \citep{robbinsmonro1951,benveniste1990} and of
solvable high-dimensional online-learning models
\citep{goldt2019dynamics,benarous2021online}. We are not aware of prior work applying
this reduction to backdoor or poisoning attack theory.

\textbf{Adaptive optimizers and rare features.} Adam \citep{kingma2015adam} and its
decoupled-weight-decay variant AdamW \citep{loshchilov2019adamw} normalize each
coordinate by a running second-moment estimate. \citet{zhang2020adaptive} attribute
Adam's advantage on attention models to heavy-tailed gradient noise, and
\citet{kunstner2024heavytailed} show that under heavy-tailed class imbalance gradient
descent makes slow progress on low-frequency classes while Adam does not. We study the
same normalization from the attacker's side: for a trigger that appears only in poison
data, it makes each poisoned batch move the trigger's parameters by a fixed amount
(Proposition~\ref{prop:adam-events}). That adaptive methods take large effective steps on rarely-active coordinates is not
new: it is the original motivation for AdaGrad~\citep{duchi2011adagrad} and follows from
the sign-like structure of Adam analysed by \citet{balles2018dissecting} and the signSGD
literature~\citep{bernstein2018signsgd}. Our contribution is not that mechanism but its
consequence for poisoning: that it makes the poisoned \emph{batch} rather than the
poisoned \emph{example} the unit of attacker resource, with a quantitative per-event
displacement and an interference scale. We are not aware of prior work drawing that
connection.

\section{Problem Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

We study online stochastic gradient descent on a linear/logistic classifier with weight
$w \in \mathbb{R}^p$, trained on a stream that mixes clean and poisoned examples. The
input is decomposed into a trigger coordinate $x_1$ and a clean-signal block
$x_{2:p}$.

\textbf{Clean distribution: a trigger is \emph{sparse}, not noisy.} A token trigger is
absent from most clean sequences, so its coordinate is exactly zero there rather than
small-but-nonzero. We model this directly. Let $q\in[0,1)$ be the trigger's clean
frequency. A clean example draws $(x_{2:p},y)$ from an arbitrary joint distribution
$\mathcal{D}_{\text{clean}}$ and then sets
\begin{equation}
x_1 = \begin{cases} c & \text{with probability } q \quad\text{(trigger present, benign continuation, } y=-1) \\ 0 & \text{with probability } 1-q \quad\text{(trigger absent)} \end{cases}
\label{eq:clean-x1}
\end{equation}
A \textbf{poisoned} example sets $x_1=c$ and assigns the attacker's label $y=+1$. Thus
$q=0$ is a trigger the clean corpus never produces, and $q>0$ one that clean text also
uses, followed there by a different token. We write
\begin{equation}
z \;=\; c\,w_1
\label{eq:zdef}
\end{equation}
for the trigger coordinate's contribution to the margin in favour of the attacker's
target, and $u = y\,w_{2:p}^\top x_{2:p}$ for the contribution of everything else. The
scalar reduction tracks $z$ only and drops $u$ on poisoned examples, where the assigned
label disagrees with the clean one; this is why the success criterion below is stated as
a required margin rather than as $z>0$, and it is the main thing the reduction gives up
relative to a full $p$-dimensional treatment (Appendix~\ref{app:highdim}).

\textbf{Why sparsity matters, and what it replaces.} For logistic loss the per-example
gradient in coordinate 1 is $-y\,x_1\,\sigma(-y\,w^\top x)$, which is \emph{proportional
to $x_1$}. When the trigger is absent, $x_1=0$ and the gradient is exactly zero --- not
zero in expectation, not zero only at $w_1=0$, but identically zero for every clean
example and every value of $w_1$. Two consequences follow, and they are why we model
absence as $x_1=0$ rather than as a small dense feature. First, at $q=0$ the trigger
coordinate is untouched by clean data along the entire trajectory, so
Proposition~\ref{thm:count-regime} below is exact rather than a small-margin
approximation. Second, this is precisely the premise
Proposition~\ref{prop:adam-events} requires (a coordinate receiving gradient only on
poisoned steps); modelling absence as dense $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ noise instead would give
the coordinate clean gradient on every step and make the two results mutually
inconsistent.

\textbf{Success criterion.} A sign criterion on $z$ is not a budget: any poison at all
makes $z>0$. The attacker's target is emitted only once the trigger's margin overcomes
the benign continuation's, so we fix a required margin $z_{\text{req}}>0$, set by the
competing clean logit, and define attack success as $z \geq z_{\text{req}}$. All
thresholds below are stated in terms of $z_{\text{req}}$.

\section{Proposition 1: The Count-Governed Regime (\texorpdfstring{$q = 0$}{q = 0})}
\label{sec:thm1}

\textbf{Claim.} Under SGD at $q=0$, clean steps contribute \emph{no} update to $w_1$, so
$w_1$ is driven by poison steps alone. Consequently (a) $z$ depends on the total poison
mass $s$ but, in the small-step limit, not on how it is divided among batches, and (b)
$z$ does not depend on the number of clean gradient steps at all.
Section~\ref{sec:prop3} shows how (a) changes under Adam.

\begin{proposition}
\label{thm:count-regime}
The per-step update $z_t = z_{t-1} + \eta c^2 \kappa_t\, \sigma(-z_{t-1})$ (where
$\kappa_t \geq 0$ is the poison mass at step $t$, zero on clean steps), in the continuum
limit $\eta c^2 \kappa_t \ll 1$, satisfies
\begin{equation}
z(s) + e^{z(s)} = 1 + \eta c^2 s, \label{eq:star}
\end{equation}
with $s = \sum_{t'\leq t}\kappa_{t'}$ the cumulative poison mass. The poison budget
required to reach $z_{\text{req}}$ is therefore
\begin{equation}
N^* \;=\; \frac{z_{\text{req}} + e^{z_{\text{req}}} - 1}{\eta c^2},
\label{eq:budget}
\end{equation}
independent of $n_{\text{clean}}$ and of the schedule, and scaling as $1/\eta$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Separating variables in $dz/ds = \eta c^2 \sigma(-z)$ gives $(1+e^z)\,dz = \eta c^2\,ds$;
integrating ($\int(1+e^z)\,dz = z+e^z$) and fixing the constant at $z(0)=0$ gives
Equation~\ref{eq:star}. Setting $z=z_{\text{req}}$ and solving for $s$ gives
Equation~\ref{eq:budget}.
\end{proof}

Equation~\ref{eq:budget} is a threshold rather than a crossover, and it makes a
prediction that a frequency-counting account does not: $N^*$ should scale inversely with
the learning rate at fixed $z_{\text{req}}$. It is the cleanest available discriminator between this model and naive counting, and we test it in Section~\ref{sec:direct}, where it is not supported as a global slope.

\textbf{Regime of validity.} The continuum approximation is accurate when each step's
perturbation is small relative to the local curvature of $\sigma(-z)$; it matches the
discrete recursion under sparse and irregular schedules and overshoots predictably when
poison is concentrated into few dense steps (Appendix~\ref{app:validity}).

\section{Proposition 2: Competing Clean Uses (\texorpdfstring{$q > 0$}{q > 0})}
\label{sec:prop2}

When $q>0$, the fraction $q$ of clean examples that contain the trigger do update $w_1$,
pushing it toward the benign continuation and opposing the poison. Because the gradient
is proportional to $x_1$, that push has magnitude $\eta c^2 q\,\sigma(z)$ per clean step
--- read directly off Equation~\ref{eq:clean-x1}, not posited.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:fraction-regime}
Let $\lambda = n_{\text{poison}}/n_{\text{clean}}$ be the poison rate among clean steps.
In the mean-field continuum limit, and neglecting $u$,
$dz/dt = \eta c^2\!\left(\lambda\,\sigma(-z) - q\,\sigma(z)\right)$, whose fixed point is
\begin{equation}
z^* = \log\!\left(\frac{\lambda}{q}\right)
    = \log\!\left(\frac{n_{\text{poison}}}{q\,n_{\text{clean}}}\right),
\label{eq:zstar}
\end{equation}
so a backdoor of any fixed required margin is implantable only if
\begin{equation}
n_{\text{poison}} \;>\; q\,n_{\text{clean}} \;=\; \text{(expected clean uses of the trigger)}.
\label{eq:sparse}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Using $\sigma(-z)=1-\sigma(z)$, the condition $\lambda\sigma(-z^*) = q\,\sigma(z^*)$
rearranges to $\sigma(z^*) = \lambda/(q+\lambda)$; solving $e^{z^*}/(1+e^{z^*})$ for this
value gives $e^{z^*}q = \lambda$, i.e.\ Equation~\ref{eq:zstar}. The right-hand side of
Equation~\ref{eq:sparse} follows by setting $z^*>0$.
\end{proof}

\textbf{This is a count, not a fraction.} The threshold is the number of competing clean
\emph{occurrences} of the trigger. It grows proportionally to $n_{\text{clean}}$ only
when the trigger's frequency $q$ is held fixed; a trigger whose absolute count stays put
as the corpus grows keeps a constant threshold. Describing the $q>0$ case as
``fraction-governed'' is therefore loose, and we avoid it: the single quantity that
matters throughout is competing clean uses, which is zero for
Proposition~\ref{thm:count-regime} and $q\,n_{\text{clean}}$ here.

\textbf{Relation to prior theory.} Existing high-dimensional treatments parameterise the
trigger as a dense feature with label overlap $\rho$ and obtain a threshold proportional
to $\rho^2 n_{\text{clean}}$~\citep{linearpoisoning2025,glmbackdoor2026}. Equating that
with Equation~\ref{eq:sparse} identifies $\rho^2/c^2 \leftrightarrow q$, so the two thresholds have the same form under that identification (a formal match, not a quantitative comparison with their results); the difference is that a sparse trigger admits
$q=0$ exactly, which a dense parameterisation cannot represent.

\textbf{Verification.} These checks establish the algebra and the continuum approximation for the scalar model, not that the scalar model describes a transformer; that is the role of Section~\ref{sec:validation}. Equation~\ref{eq:zstar} was checked against the discrete recursion at $q\in\{0.05,0.2,0.5\}$, $n_{\text{clean}}\in\{4{,}000,16{,}000\}$ and
$n_{\text{poison}}\in\{200,800,3200\}$: the predicted side of the threshold is correct in
all 14 cells away from it, and the 4 cells sitting exactly at $z^*=0$ show the same
$O(\eta)$ discretisation bias as Proposition~\ref{thm:count-regime}, shrinking linearly in $\eta$ (Figure~\ref{fig:theory}b and Table~\ref{tab:sparse}; Appendix~\ref{app:sparse}). We note that checking a closed form against the
recursion it was derived from is a consistency check, not independent validation;
Appendix~\ref{app:highdim} reports the corresponding $p=200$ simulation, and
Sections~\ref{sec:validation} and~\ref{sec:realtext} test Equation~\ref{eq:sparse}
against transformers, where $q$ is measured rather than imposed.

\section{Proposition 3: Adaptive Optimizers Count Poison Events}
\label{sec:prop3}

Proposition~\ref{thm:count-regime} assumes SGD, under which a poisoned step moves the
trigger weight in proportion to the number of poison examples it contains. Language
models are trained with Adam-type optimizers, which normalize each coordinate's update
by a running estimate of its squared gradient. At $q=0$ the trigger's parameters receive gradient \emph{only} on poisoned steps, exactly (Equation~\ref{eq:clean-x1}), which is exactly the regime in which
this normalization matters most. Consider one such coordinate $\theta$ under Adam
with momentum $\beta_1$, second-moment decay $\beta_2$, learning rate $\eta$ and
$\beta_1<\sqrt{\beta_2}$ (as for the standard $0.9, 0.999$). A \emph{poison event} is a
step whose batch contains at least one poison example, giving gradient $g_n\neq0$;
all other steps give zero gradient to $\theta$.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:adam-events}
Let $E$ poison events occur with spacing $\Delta \gg 1/(1-\beta_1)$ and equal gradient
magnitude $|g_n|=g$ (the small-margin regime), and neglect bias correction and weight
decay. Then the total displacement of $\theta$ is
\begin{equation}
z_E \;=\; D_0\,\sqrt{1-\beta_2^{\Delta}}\;\sum_{n=1}^{E}\bigl(1-\beta_2^{n\Delta}\bigr)^{-1/2},
\qquad
D_0 \;=\; \frac{\eta\,(1-\beta_1)}{\bigl(1-\beta_1/\sqrt{\beta_2}\bigr)\sqrt{1-\beta_2}},
\label{eq:adam}
\end{equation}
which does not depend on $g$, and hence not on how many poison examples each event
contains. In particular, $z_E \to E\,D_0$ when $\Delta\gg1/(1-\beta_2)$ (every
event counts fully), and $z_E \approx 2D_0\sqrt{E}$ when $E\Delta\ll1/(1-\beta_2)$ --- the condition is on the
\emph{total} span $E\Delta$, not the spacing alone, since the $n$-th term is suppressed
only while $n\Delta$ remains inside the second-moment memory (clustered events count as
$\sqrt{E}$).
\end{proposition}

\emph{Proof in Appendix~\ref{app:prop3proof}.} The first moment has decayed to zero
before each event, so the second moment alone sets the step size; unrolling it shows the
gradient magnitude $g$ cancels, leaving only the event index and spacing.

\textbf{Verification.} Simulating the Adam recursion directly (with $\epsilon=0$, no bias correction and no weight decay), a single event moves $\theta$ by $31.8\,\eta$ whether the gradient scale corresponds to 1, 4, 16 or 64 poison examples (\texttt{adam\_single\_event\_check.csv}).
Across spacings from 200 to 10{,}000 steps and 4 to 16 events,
Equation~\ref{eq:adam} matches the simulated displacement to within $0.01\%$, degrading to $0.8\%$ at spacing 20 where momentum tails overlap (Appendix~\ref{app:prop3proof}).

\textbf{When $g$ does not cancel.} The cancellation requires the second-moment estimate
to be dominated by the poison gradient rather than by Adam's $\epsilon$, i.e.\
$g\sqrt{1-\beta_2}\gg\epsilon$. A single poison example in a large batch contributes a
batch-averaged gradient that can violate this, in which case the per-event displacement
falls below $D_0$ and scales with $g$ again --- so the batch-counting effect should
weaken, not strengthen, at very low per-batch density. Equation~\ref{eq:adam} also
assumes equal $|g_n|$; because $g_n\propto\sigma(-z)$, later events carry smaller
gradients once the margin has grown, which makes Equation~\ref{eq:adam} an upper bound
on late-event contributions and predicts saturation in $E$ beyond what the formula
shows. Finally, bias correction is not negligible for events very early in training
($1/(1-\beta_2^{5})\approx200$ at step 5), which is one reason the early-placement
condition of Section~\ref{sec:validation} is not a clean test of the formula.

\textbf{Consequence: two channels.} Proposition~\ref{prop:adam-events} applies to
trigger-specific parameters, such as the trigger's embedding, which see gradient only on
poisoned steps. Parameters shared with the clean task have their second moment set by
clean gradients; for them Adam acts like SGD with a rescaled step, and a poisoned batch
moves them in proportion to the poison it contains. Under Adam the attack therefore, on this account, draws on two resources -- poisoned batches through the trigger-specific channel
(discounted within $1/(1-\beta_2)$ steps of one another) and poison examples through the
shared channel -- whereas under SGD both count examples. The resulting prediction, which
SGD does not make, is that at fixed total poison, spreading it over more batches
increases attack success. Simulating the scalar Adam recursion under each transformer
condition's exact schedule, with no fitted parameter, ranks the 11 conditions against
measured ASR at Spearman $0.67$ ($p=0.023$; Appendix~\ref{app:adam}). We report this as weak, suggestive evidence only; the pool also includes the three placement conditions, between which our own placement experiment finds no significant difference, so part of the correlation is between conditions we cannot distinguish empirically: the 11 conditions pool three heterogeneous families
(training lengths, schedules, placements) whose displacement-to-ASR mapping is evidently
not shared --- a predicted displacement of $0.052$ accompanies ASR $0.52$ while $0.149$
accompanies $0.13$ --- and the conditions are not independent. Within families the picture is mixed: for the five training lengths the correlation is $0.40$ (not significant at $n=5$, and the predicted displacement is higher at $T=32{,}000$ than at $T=2{,}000$ while ASR is lower), and for the three schedules and three placements it is $1.0$ but on three points each. The pooled value is therefore carried largely by differences between families. A pooled rank correlation over such groups cannot establish the mechanism; the controlled comparison of
Section~\ref{sec:events} is what the claim rests on.

\textbf{Idealizations.} Equation~\ref{eq:adam} omits Adam's $\epsilon$, bias correction,
and parameters that process the trigger jointly with clean tokens; it also predicts
interference between poisoned batches spaced within $1/(1-\beta_2)$ steps, which is
compatible with the reported insensitivity to poisoned-batch frequency because typical
pretraining spacings are far wider than that window; two $\beta_2$ tests of the interference prediction found no effect (Section~\ref{sec:direct}), so this compatibility argument rests on a prediction we withdraw. Appendix~\ref{app:idealize}
discusses both points and what they predict.

\section{Real-Transformer Validation}
\label{sec:validation}

We test the theory's predictions on a transformer trained from scratch, sweeping the
axes studied by \citet{souly2025poisoning}: how poison is scheduled, how long clean
training runs, and whether the trigger also occurs in clean data.

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup-exp}

\textbf{Task and model.} Clean sequences of length 32 are drawn from a fixed bigram
language model over a 64-token vocabulary (plus one reserved id for the trigger): the first token is uniform, and each
subsequent token is sampled from $\mathrm{softmax}(B_{x_{t-1},:})$ with
$B_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ drawn once and shared by all runs. The model is a
causally-masked transformer \citep{vaswani2017attention} with 4 layers, width 128, 4 heads, feed-forward width 512
and learned positional embeddings (0.81M parameters), trained with next-token
cross-entropy and AdamW \citep{loshchilov2019adamw} (learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $0.01$, batch 64).
Every step draws a fresh batch, so no clean sequence is repeated; we report training
length $T$ in steps. Each run uses a fresh random initialization.

\textbf{Poisoning.} The trigger is a dedicated token (id 64) that the bigram model never
produces. A poison example is a clean sequence with the trigger written at position
16 and the attacker's target token at position 17; poison examples replace clean
sequences within a batch. A schedule is specified by the total poison count $N$ and
the number of poison examples per poisoned batch (the \emph{density}); poisoned
batches are spaced evenly from step 5 to step $T-5$.

\textbf{Attack success rate (ASR).} After training, we place the trigger at position
16 of 200 fresh uniformly random sequences and report the fraction for which the
model's most likely next token is the target.

\textbf{Rare versus generic trigger.} In the rare condition the trigger never appears in clean data ($q=0$). In the generic condition a fraction of \emph{clean}
sequences contain the same trigger in the same slot followed by a fixed benign token,
at an average of 0.1 occurrences per step. This mirrors the poison exactly except for
the continuation, so clean and poison signals compete for the same prediction, which
is the situation Proposition~\ref{prop:fraction-regime} models. Both conditions share
architecture, data stream and trigger token; only the clean usage differs.

\textbf{What this design can and cannot distinguish.} We flag a limitation of the test
itself, not of its execution. Because clean and poison uses occupy the same slot and
differ only in the following token, Equation~\ref{eq:sparse}'s threshold coincides with
the point at which poison continuations outnumber benign ones after that token --- which
is where the argmax of the empirical conditional distribution flips. Any model that
fits conditional next-token frequencies will therefore reproduce the same ordering
without appealing to the dynamics, so this experiment is consistent with the threshold being the competing-use count (to within $1.0$--$1.2\times$; at $T=1{,}000$ the bootstrap interval excludes the exact prediction) but does not discriminate
Proposition~\ref{prop:fraction-regime} from that simpler account. Distinguishing them
needs a prediction frequency counting does not make. Equation~\ref{eq:budget} supplies
one ($N^*\propto1/\eta$ at fixed required margin) and Proposition~\ref{prop:adam-events}
supplies another (interference on a $1/(1-\beta_2)$ scale). A learning-rate sweep (Section~\ref{sec:direct}) did not follow the first as a global slope, and two $\beta_2$ tests did not support the second.

\subsection{Results}

\textbf{Training length (Figure~\ref{fig:realmodel}a).} With $N=16$ poison examples
at density 4, ASR is not constant in training length. Pooling two independent sweeps
(20 runs per length up to $32{,}000$; 6 at $64{,}000$), mean ASR is $0.10$ at
$T=500$, $0.52$ at $2{,}000$, $0.49$ at $8{,}000$, $0.18$ at $32{,}000$ and $0.13$ at
$64{,}000$. Comparing $T=500$ with the pooled $T\in\{2{,}000,8{,}000\}$ runs, and those with the pooled $T\in\{32{,}000,64{,}000\}$ runs (windows chosen after seeing the means), one-sided Mann--Whitney gives $p=7\times10^{-5}$ and $p=6\times10^{-4}$; single-length contrasts are weaker ($T=8{,}000$ versus $32{,}000$: $p=0.012$; versus $64{,}000$: $p=0.059$, 6 runs); the two sweeps agree on this shape but not
on whether the peak lies at 2{,}000 or 8{,}000. Proposition~\ref{thm:count-regime}'s
invariance therefore holds only approximately, within a window of training lengths. A
caveat on this comparison: Proposition~\ref{thm:count-regime} is an SGD result, whereas
every run in this sweep uses AdamW, so the mismatch may reflect the optimizer rather
than a failure of the proposition. The corresponding SGD sweep over $T$ is not reported
here and is needed to separate the two. The decline is not forgetting after the final
dose, because the last poisoned batch is always 5 steps before the end of training.
Instead, the spacing between the four poisoned batches grows as $T/3$, consistent
with each dose partially decaying before the next arrives. The Adam account of Section~\ref{sec:prop3} reproduces this shape once decoupled
weight decay is included: simulating the trigger coordinate under each experiment's
own schedule gives a displacement whose maximum falls at $T=8{,}000$, inside the observed peak
region, while setting the weight decay to zero makes it rise and then plateau ($0.082$ at both $T=32{,}000$ and $64{,}000$) with no peak (Appendix~\ref{app:adam}). The predicted decline is far smaller than the
observed one ($17\%$ against $75\%$ from $T=8{,}000$ to $64{,}000$), so decoupled
weight decay accounts for the turning point and the direction but not the magnitude.
A rise-times-decay consistency check is reported in Appendix~\ref{app:fit}.

\textbf{Scheduling (Figure~\ref{fig:realmodel}b), and why this sweep does not support a
batch-count claim.} This sweep varies batch count by varying per-batch density at fixed
$N=16$ and $T=3{,}000$, and obtains mean ASR $0.53$ for 16 batches, $0.23$ for 4, and
$0$ in all 5 seeds for a single batch. We report it for completeness but do \emph{not}
rest any conclusion on it, because in this sweep batch count and poison \emph{placement}
are not separable: the schedule generator spaces poisoned batches evenly between step 5
and step $T-5$, so reducing the batch count also moves the poison earlier. At one batch
the single poisoned step lands at step 5, followed by $2{,}995$ clean steps --- the
longest possible exposure to the erosion documented above. The apparent batch-count effect here may therefore be partly, and at one batch perhaps entirely, an early-placement effect; we have not shown that it is (see below).

\textbf{Reconciling Figure~\ref{fig:realmodel}b with Section~\ref{sec:events}.} The same
nominal condition (AdamW, $N=16$, $T=3{,}000$) appears in both sweeps with different
results: $0.00$ versus $0.376$ at one batch, $0.23$ versus $0.724$ at four, and $0.53$
versus $0.841$ at sixteen. We have \emph{not} established the cause of this discrepancy. The two sweeps differ in where the single poisoned batch lands (step 5 versus step $1{,}500$, a difference of nearly $1{,}500$ clean steps of subsequent training) and in whether network initialisation is seeded (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}); these are confounded with each other, and with 5 seeds the one-batch contrast alone ($0/5$ versus $2/5$ successes) is not significant (Fisher exact $p=0.44$). Section~\ref{sec:events} holds the span of training over which poison is delivered fixed while varying only how the budget is divided, so it is the more controlled comparison, and the batch-count claim rests on it alone. At 20 seeds Section~\ref{sec:events}'s configuration gives mean ASR $0.22$, $0.51$ and $0.67$ for 1, 4 and 16 batches, between the two earlier sets; gaps of this size are what 5 seeds can produce (zero successes in five runs has probability $0.24$ at a true rate of $0.25$), though the generators also differ in placement. A single-protocol rerun with seeded initialisation would resolve the discrepancy; until then Figure~\ref{fig:realmodel}b should be read as uninformative about batch count.

\textbf{Placement ($N=16$, $T=32{,}000$, 5 runs per condition).} Sixteen single-example poisoned batches are placed in the first $10\%$ of training (early), evenly from step 5 to $T-5$ (even), or in the last $10\%$ (late). Mean ASR is $0.13$, $0.55$ and $0.29$. No pairwise difference is significant (two-sided Mann--Whitney: early versus late $p=1.00$, early versus even $p=0.22$, late versus even $p=0.30$), so this experiment neither supports nor excludes an explanation in terms of erosion after the final dose.

\textbf{Rare versus generic trigger (fixed in advance).} Proposition~\ref{prop:fraction-regime} predicts
that the poison count needed to flip the prediction grows in proportion to the number
of competing clean uses, while the rare trigger's threshold does not depend on
training length. By Equation~\ref{eq:sparse}, $z^*>0$ exactly when poison examples outnumber competing clean uses; at
0.1 clean uses per step this predicts thresholds near 100, 300 and 900 poison examples at $T=1{,}000$, $3{,}000$ and $9{,}000$; we fixed this prediction
before running the sweep ($N\in\{25,75,225,675,2025\}$, density 8, 3 seeds per cell).
The result is consistent with the prediction (Figure~\ref{fig:rho}). For the generic trigger, the
\emph{interpolated threshold estimate} for 50\% ASR (log-linear between grid points; 3
seeds per cell) is 123, 346
and 914 at $T=1{,}000$, $3{,}000$ and $9{,}000$ (bootstrap 95\% intervals over seeds
$[115,130]$, $[239,383]$ and $[407,1173]$), or 1.23, 1.15 and 1.02 times the expected
number of clean uses of the trigger. The bootstrap resamples seeds on a fixed grid whose points are 3$\times$ apart, so it does not capture interpolation error between grid points, and these intervals are narrower than the true uncertainty; that at $T=1{,}000$ the interval excludes the exact prediction of 100 should be read in that light. The rare-trigger thresholds at $T=1{,}000$ and $3{,}000$ lie below the smallest grid point ($N=25$) and are censored, so they bound the threshold above and say nothing about its variation with $T$. Fitting a log--log slope against $T$ gives $0.91$ (95\% interval
$[0.54,1.04]$; predicted 1). With three training lengths and three seeds per cell we
treat this slope as descriptive rather than as a precise estimate of the exponent: the
claim we make is that the three lengths are consistent with the predicted linear
scaling, not that the exponent is resolved. For the rare trigger, mean ASR at the
smallest budget of 25 poison examples is already $0.67$ and $0.83$ at $T=1{,}000$ and
$3{,}000$, so the threshold lies below the grid; at $T=9{,}000$ it is 39 (interval
$[33,42]$), 23 times lower than for the generic trigger at the same length. The rare
threshold rises slightly at the longest length, in line with the training-length
decline in Figure~\ref{fig:realmodel}a, but grows far less than the ninefold
increase in clean data.

\subsection{Controlled Optimizer Intervention: AdamW versus SGD}
\label{sec:events}

Section~\ref{sec:validation}'s conditions vary the schedule but not the optimizer, so the
batch-count effect there is a correlation. To test it directly, we train the same
transformer with SGD (momentum $0.9$, learning rate $0.1$, tuned to match AdamW's
clean-data loss) instead of AdamW and hold the total poison count $N$ fixed while varying
how many batches $E$ it is split into, at $T=3{,}000$.
Proposition~\ref{prop:adam-events} predicts that more batches raise attack success under
AdamW and not under SGD. An earlier 5-seed version of this experiment gave AdamW $2/5$,
$5/5$ and $5/5$ successes at 1, 4 and 16 batches; that estimate was optimistic, and we
report the 20-seed run, which supersedes it at $N=16$ (Figure~\ref{fig:events} still shows the earlier run).

At $N=16$ (Table~\ref{tab:events20}), AdamW succeeds (ASR $>0.5$) in $5/20$ runs at 1 batch
$[0.11,0.47]$, $11/20$ at 4 and $13/20$ at 16 $[0.43,0.82]$, with mean ASR $0.22$, $0.51$
and $0.67$. The seed fixes both initialisation and the clean data stream, independent of
$E$, so runs are paired across batch counts. Of the 8 seeds whose outcome differs between 1
and 16 batches, all 8 succeed only at 16, and none succeeds at 1 batch and fails at 16
(exact McNemar $p=0.008$; unpaired Fisher $p=0.025$). Two comparisons were designated in
advance, AdamW and SGD at 1 versus 16 batches, and the $p$-values are uncorrected for those
two; 1 versus 4 batches (paired $p=0.031$) is a further comparison. The Wilson intervals at 1
and 16 batches just touch, so we describe the effect as supported by the paired test and
modest in sample size, not as large. The same seeds rerun on different hardware did not
reproduce per-seed outcomes (one seed went from $0.02$ to $0.58$ ASR at one batch), so
outcomes near the threshold are sensitive and the earlier 5-seed means ($0.38$, $0.72$,
$0.84$) were higher than seeds 5--19 give ($2/15$, $8/15$, $10/15$ successes).

\textbf{The SGD arm is at floor, and bounds rather than establishes the contrast.} SGD
succeeds in $1/20$ runs at every batch count, and it is the same seed each time. A null
effect measured at the floor cannot distinguish ``batch count does not matter under SGD''
from ``SGD cannot implant this backdoor at this budget at all'' --- and the latter is
plausible, since rare-feature learning under SGD is known to be slow relative to adaptive
methods~\citep{duchi2011adagrad,kunstner2024heavytailed}. We then ran SGD away from the floor, at $N=64$ and $N=128$ over 1 to 16 batches
(6 seeds; Table~\ref{tab:matched}). Mean ASR is $0.25$--$0.33$ at $N=64$ and $0.83$ at $N=128$ for every batch
count, and at $N=128$ each seed gives the same outcome at every batch count (seed 0 fails
throughout, the other five succeed), so batch count has no visible effect under SGD at either
budget (Fisher $p=1$ for 1 against 16 batches at $N=64$). This is a stronger statement of the
SGD arm than floor data allowed. It is not a matched-difficulty comparison: AdamW at the same
budgets succeeds in 6 of 6 seeds at every batch count, so its response to batch count cannot be
read there, and the optimizer contrast rests on different budgets ($N=16$ for AdamW, $N=64$--$128$
for SGD). We chose these budgets knowing AdamW was already near ceiling at $N=64$ in earlier
data, which was a design error. The SGD learning rate was tuned to match
AdamW's clean-data loss, not its attack dynamics, so the arms are matched on optimisation
quality and not on attack strength. In the earlier 5-seed run, $N=4$ and $N=256$ sit near
floor and ceiling respectively, so no separation is possible there.

\subsection{Direct Tests of the Adam Mechanism}
\label{sec:direct}

\textbf{Embedding displacement (fixed in advance; supported).} Proposition~\ref{prop:adam-events}
rests on the premise that a poisoned batch moves the trigger's own embedding row by an amount
independent of how many poison examples it contains. We measure it: after 1{,}000 clean steps we
apply one poisoned batch of $k\in\{1,4,16,64\}$ poison examples and record the change in the L2
norm of the trigger's embedding row across that step (5 seeds per cell; Table~\ref{tab:embdisp}).
Under AdamW the displacement is $0.0050$ to $0.0051$ for all $k$ at $\beta_2=0.95$ against a closed
form of $0.00506$, and likewise flat at $\beta_2=0.99$ ($0.0111$--$0.0113$ against $0.0113$) and
$\beta_2=0.999$ ($0.0276$--$0.0284$ against $0.0285$; at $k=1$ it is $3\%$ low, the $\epsilon$ effect
noted in the appendix). Under SGD it grows in proportion to $k$: $0.00003$, $0.00013$, $0.00052$ and
$0.00208$ for $k=1,4,16,64$ ($63.6\times$ for $64\times$ the poison). This confirms the premise; it
does not show that the premise produces the attack-success effect.

\textbf{$\beta_2$ and interference (fixed in advance; not supported, twice).}
Proposition~\ref{prop:adam-events} predicts that poisoned batches closer together than
$1/(1-\beta_2)$ interfere. A first test (10 seeds, Table~\ref{tab:beta2}) found no effect,
and its design confounded step size with interference, because the per-event step grows with
$\beta_2$ (Table~\ref{tab:embdisp}). We therefore reran it with the trigger row's learning rate
set per $\beta_2$ to hold the step fixed, $\beta_2$ and learning rate applied to that row only,
and the poisoned block placed late in a 12{,}000-step run so Adam's bias correction is
negligible ($N=32$ in 8 batches of 4; $\beta_2\in\{0.9,0.95,0.99,0.999\}$ crossed with spacing
$\{10,50,250\}$; 8 seeds). The ratio of the poisoned block's span to Adam's memory ranges from $0.07$ to $175$, with three cells below 1 (Table~\ref{tab:int2}). The experiment records the displacement it produces,
and our pre-specified validity check (the window displacement differing by under $1.3\times$
across $\beta_2$) passed, at $1.14\times$. The displacement across the single step after the
first event is not matched, differing up to $1.9\times$, since momentum and the second moment
act over the following steps; the window quantity is the one that sets total movement. Mean ASR
is mid-range throughout ($0.66$--$0.87$), so neither floor nor ceiling limits the contrast, and
cells inside the interference window do not fall: $0.71$ against $0.78$ outside (difference
$-0.07$, one-sided Mann--Whitney $p=0.16$). Between $\beta_2=0.9$ and $0.999$ the change in mean
ASR is $+0.00$, $-0.21$ and $-0.06$ at spacing 10, 50 and 250 ($p=0.83$, $0.20$, $0.71$). The direction is as predicted at two of three
spacings, but small and not significant at 8 seeds, which cannot exclude a moderate effect. Under
the rule fixed in advance the interference prediction is withdrawn as an empirical claim; the
algebra of the scalar recursion is unaffected.

\textbf{Learning rate (fixed in advance; not supported as a global slope).}
Equation~\ref{eq:budget} predicts $N^*\propto1/\eta$ where frequency counting predicts no
dependence. At learning rates $3\times10^{-4}$, $10^{-3}$ and $3\times10^{-3}$ (5 seeds, Table~\ref{tab:lrsweep}), $N^*=52.6$ $[39.4,81.8]$, $15.8$ $[12.1,22.2]$ and $39.2$
$[13.3,83.4]$: a log-log slope of $-0.14$ against a predicted $-1$ (accepted range
$-0.7$ to $-1.3$). The first two points do fall by $3.3\times$ as the learning rate rises
$3.3\times$, but the third reverses, with success not reaching $1.0$ until $N=256$. A likely
cause is unstable training at the highest rate, but clean loss was not recorded in this sweep,
so we could not check, and the two-point agreement was noticed after the third point was seen.
We do not claim the $1/\eta$ budget.

\textbf{Parameter groups (20 seeds; supported at $N=16$).} We repeated $N\in\{16,32\}$ and
$E\in\{1,4,16\}$ with the trigger's embedding row in its own optimizer group, pooling the
first 5 seeds with 15 further seeds for 20 per cell (Table~\ref{tab:pgroups}). The unsplit
AdamW control and the split arm agree (at $N=16$, mean ASR $0.23$, $0.51$, $0.70$ against
$0.23$, $0.51$, $0.70$ for 1, 4, 16 batches), so the split does not change the dynamics.
We predicted that batch count matters only when the trigger row is on AdamW. At $N=16$ with
the row on AdamW, successes rise from $5/20$ to $15/20$ between 1 and 16 batches
(Fisher $p=0.004$); with the row on SGD and the rest of the network on AdamW they rise from
$3/20$ to $7/20$ ($p=0.27$), a smaller rise in mean ASR ($+0.17$ against $+0.46$; Mann--Whitney
$p=0.003$ for the difference between arms), and at 16 batches the arms differ, $15/20$ against
$7/20$ ($p=0.025$). With the network on SGD and only the row on AdamW, successes rise from
$2/20$ to $9/20$ ($p=0.031$), so the row on AdamW is enough to produce a batch-count effect in
an otherwise-SGD network. At $N=32$ the arms are at or near ceiling ($17/20$ at 16 batches in
both) and do not differ, so those cells say nothing about the mechanism. The remaining rise with
the row on SGD ($3/20$ to $7/20$) is not significant but is not zero, and the two-channel account
does not predict it. The 8 contrasts examined are uncorrected.

\subsection{Model Size and Per-Batch Density}
\label{sec:threshold}

These two sweeps were specified, with their predictions and the rule for each outcome,
before they ran. Both estimate the poison count $N^*$ at which mean ASR crosses $0.5$ by
log-linear interpolation over a grid of $N$ (8 seeds per cell), with seed-bootstrap
intervals.

\textbf{Model size.} Neither proposition contains a parameter-count term, so we predicted
$N^*$ flat in model size (log-log slope within $\pm0.15$). We trained four widths and
depths from $1.1\times10^5$ to $2.5\times10^7$ parameters on the same task
(Table~\ref{tab:modelsize}). From $0.8$M to $25$M parameters, a $31\times$ range,
$N^*=12.7$ $[10.0,18.8]$, $11.5$ $[10.4,12.6]$ and $11.6$ $[9.4,14.9]$, a slope of $-0.02$
(bootstrap interval $[-0.15,0.08]$). The invariance does not extend below that range: the
$0.11$M-parameter model reaches mean ASR $0.46$ at $N=64$ (5 of 8 seeds above $0.5$), so its
$N^*$ exceeds $64$, more than five times the others, and including it as a lower bound
makes the slope at most $-0.30$. Two cautions apply. First, the $25$M model trained at the
common learning rate $10^{-3}$ did not learn the clean task (clean loss $4.147$ against
$3.71$--$3.73$ for every other cell, and $\ln 65=4.17$ for a uniform guess) and never
reached non-zero ASR; the pre-specified guard excludes a cell whose clean task fails, so
the $25$M point is the one trained at $3\times10^{-4}$, where clean loss matches. That
point therefore differs in learning rate from the other three, and we have not isolated
the effect of learning rate at that size. Second, the range tops out at $25$M parameters,
against the $600$M--$13$B of \citet{souly2025poisoning}, on a synthetic bigram task, so
this shows invariance over a small range in a controlled setting rather than reproducing
their scale result.

\textbf{Per-batch density.} Proposition~\ref{prop:adam-events} makes the trigger row's
displacement per poisoned batch independent of how many poison examples the batch holds,
so reaching a fixed number of poisoned batches costs $N^*\propto k$ (slope $1$; we
accepted $0.8$--$1.2$ as support), whereas an account in which poison examples are the
resource predicts slope $0$. At $k=1,2,4,8,16$ examples per batch we find
$N^*=7.0$, $9.4$, $11.8$, $14.7$ and $23.8$, a slope of $0.42$ with bootstrap interval
$[0.23,0.55]$ (Table~\ref{tab:density}). That interval excludes both $1$ and $0$. The
number of poisoned batches needed falls from $7.0$ at $k=1$ to $1.5$ at $k=16$, so denser
batches do substitute for batch count, but only partly. This reproduces in direction the
density effect of \citet{souly2025poisoning}, and quantifies it as sublinear. It is what
the two-channel account of Section~\ref{sec:prop3} allows, with trigger-specific
parameters counting batches and shared parameters counting examples, but it is also
what any mixture of the two would give, so we regard it as inconsistent with either
single-resource account and not as a test of the two-channel account in particular.
At $k=16$ the interpolation brackets $N=16$ (a single batch, ASR $0.01$) and $N=32$
(ASR $0.86$), so that estimate is coarse.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:disc}

\textbf{What the theory explains.} The trigger's overlap with clean data decides
whether the poison budget is set by a count or by a fraction of the data
(Proposition~\ref{thm:count-regime}, Proposition~\ref{prop:fraction-regime}). Under Adam,
trigger-specific parameters additionally count poisoned batches
(Proposition~\ref{prop:adam-events}), which accounts for the observation of
\citet{souly2025poisoning} that denser poisoned batches need more poison samples, and is consistent with our suggestive, 5-seed observation that spreading a fixed budget over more batches raises attack success.

\textbf{Horizon.} Proposition~\ref{thm:count-regime} describes a finite-horizon regime: it holds while
the trigger's margin is small and no clean gradient reaches the trigger's own parameters. Real
networks share parameters between the trigger and clean tokens, so clean training can erode an
implanted backdoor over long horizons; we take this to be the likely source of the decline in
Figure~\ref{fig:realmodel}a and we do not model it. The count regime should therefore be read as a
statement about the poison needed to implant a backdoor within a fixed training budget, not as an
invariance that holds at every horizon.

\textbf{What it explains only partly.} Attack success on the transformer declines for
training runs much longer than the poison schedule (Figure~\ref{fig:realmodel}a). This
is not forgetting after the final poisoned batch, and in a dedicated placement experiment (5 runs per condition) no pairwise difference between early, even and late placement of the poison was significant (two-sided Mann--Whitney $p\geq0.22$), so an erosion-after-dosing account is neither supported nor excluded. Two mechanisms in
our model point the right way without the right magnitude: with decoupled weight decay
the Adam model's maximum falls at $T=8{,}000$, within the observed peak region of
$2{,}000$--$8{,}000$ (whose two means, $0.52$ and $0.49$, we cannot separate), and
declines thereafter, while removing the weight decay removes the decline entirely;. Neither
reproduces the size of the decline, $17\%$ predicted against $75\%$ observed.
\citet{souly2025poisoning} likewise report gradual decay under continued clean training.
Closing the gap plausibly requires modelling the drift of parameters shared with the
clean task, which the scalar reduction does not track.

\textbf{Scope.} The from-scratch experiments use a synthetic bigram task and a 0.81M-parameter transformer (Section~\ref{sec:threshold} varies this from 0.11M to 25M parameters), following prior solvable-model poisoning
theory~\citep{linearpoisoning2025,glmbackdoor2026}; Section~\ref{sec:realtext} adds a pretrained
model on natural text at two seeds per cell. The theory is a scalar reduction. A $p=200$ simulation
(Appendix~\ref{app:highdim}) finds that its orderings in $N$ and $\rho$ hold in every cell and in $T$ in 15 of 18, but also that Proposition~\ref{prop:fraction-regime} overestimates attack success at $\rho>0$ there (sign agreement 16 of 24), so we read it as consistent with the mechanism and the direction of the dependence rather than a quantitatively accurate threshold. Individual runs end close to 0 or 1, so we report success counts with Wilson intervals
where it matters. The optimizer and parameter-group comparisons rest on 20 seeds, the $\beta_2$ tests on 10 and 8, the learning-rate sweep on 5 and the SGD sweep on 6. The experiment that would most change what can be concluded is an SGD sweep over training length, to test Proposition~\ref{thm:count-regime} under the optimizer it assumes rather than under AdamW; a same-budget AdamW-versus-SGD comparison also remains undone, since our attempt put AdamW at ceiling.

\section{Conclusion}

What a backdoor costs is set, in the model studied here, by one quantity: the number of
times clean data already uses the trigger. For a token the corpus never produces that
number is zero, and the budget is a count; for one clean text also uses it is
$q\,n_{\text{clean}}$. A trigger the corpus
never produces is learned from poison alone while its margin is small, so additional
clean data does not raise the threshold; a trigger clean text also uses must outcompete
those uses, and its price grows with the corpus. Applied to Adam, the same model accounts for
why packing poison densely into batches wastes it. For defenders this reframes what to
measure: corpus frequency of a candidate trigger, not poison fraction, orders how expensive the attack is in our experiments. The clearest open problem is quantitative -- both of our decay
mechanisms underpredict how fast an implanted backdoor erodes under long clean training.

\subsection{Protocol and Scope for the Pretrained-Model Experiment}
\label{app:realtext}

\textbf{Setup.} We tokenize the corpus, count every token, and select triggers whose
\emph{observed} frequency lands near four targets, including one token the corpus never produces ($q=0$). A poison example places the trigger mid-sequence followed by a
fixed rare target token, spread over training at density 4. Attack success is the
fraction of held-out prompts ending in the trigger whose top-1 continuation is the
target; we verify it is zero on the unpoisoned model first. Clean validation loss is
recorded for every run.


\textbf{Scope and confounds.} The model is pretrained rather than trained from scratch,
the corpus is natural text, and trigger frequencies are measured rather than imposed, so
the $q$ transition is tested outside the setting that produced it. It remains a small
model on a small corpus at two seeds per cell, with the highest-frequency condition
incomplete at submission; we do not reproduce the pretraining-scale behaviour of
\citet{souly2025poisoning} and do not claim to. A specific confound deserves naming: we
measure $q$ in WikiText-2, not in the Pile that Pythia was pretrained on, so a token
absent from WikiText may still have been seen in pretraining, and conversely a token
rare in the Pile may be undertrained in the checkpoint in ways that make it unusually
easy to repurpose~\citep{land2024magikarp}. Measuring $q$ against the pretraining corpus
is the correct control and we have not done it, so the ordering we report should be read
as consistent with Equation~\ref{eq:sparse} rather than as a clean test of it.


\subsection{Idealizations in Proposition~\ref{prop:adam-events}}
\label{app:idealize}

\textbf{Idealizations, and the apparent tension with reported frequency-invariance.}
Equation~\ref{eq:adam} omits Adam's $\epsilon$, bias correction, and any parameters that
process the trigger jointly with clean tokens. The $\epsilon$ omission matters where the
averaged gradient is small: a single poison example in a large batch can give a
per-coordinate gradient comparable to $\epsilon$, at which point the normalization no
longer cancels $g$ and the per-batch displacement falls below $D_0$. This is one reason
to expect the batch-counting effect to weaken at very low per-batch density.
Proposition~\ref{prop:adam-events} also predicts interference between poisoned batches
spaced closer than $1/(1-\beta_2)$ steps, which may appear to conflict with
\citet{souly2025poisoning}'s report that the \emph{frequency} of poisoned batches did not
change the poison needed at fixed density. The two are compatible: at
$\beta_2=0.999$ the interference window is $\approx10^3$ steps, and in a pretraining run
of $10^5$--$10^6$ steps with a few hundred poisoned documents, successive poisoned
batches are typically separated by far more than that, so each acts independently and
only their count matters. The prediction is that frequency becomes relevant once poisoned
batches are packed within $\sim1/(1-\beta_2)$ steps of one another, a regime that
reported sweeps have not probed.


\subsection{Protocol Differences and Seeding}
\label{app:protocol}

Two generators are used. \texttt{spread\_schedule} (Figure~\ref{fig:realmodel}b) places
$\lceil N/d\rceil$ poisoned batches of density $d$ evenly between step 5 and step $T-5$,
so batch count and placement covary. \texttt{events\_schedule}
(Section~\ref{sec:events}) places $E$ poisoned batches at the centres of $E$ equal
segments of training, holding the delivery span fixed as $E$ varies. At $N=16$,
$T=3{,}000$ the first poisoned step is at 5 versus 1{,}500 for one batch, 5 versus 375
for four, and 5 versus 93 for sixteen.

Seeding differs as well: the \texttt{events} runs seed network initialisation
(\texttt{torch.manual\_seed}) as well as data sampling, while the training-length,
scheduling, trigger-overlap, placement and calibration runs seed only data sampling, so
their initialisations vary run to run. This inflates their between-run variance relative
to the \texttt{events} runs and is a defect of those sweeps; the conclusions we draw
from them are limited to the effects that survive it, and the per-run outcomes are
released so this can be checked.

\textbf{Optimiser and architecture details.} All transformer runs use a constant
learning rate with no warmup or decay; AdamW with $(\beta_1,\beta_2)=(0.9,0.999)$,
$\epsilon=10^{-8}$ and weight decay $0.01$ unless stated; SGD with momentum $0.9$. The
token embedding and the output projection are \emph{separate} parameters (untied), so
the trigger's embedding row is a genuinely trigger-specific parameter, as
Proposition~\ref{prop:adam-events} requires. With tied weights the premise would fail,
since the output row for the target token receives gradient on every step through the
softmax.

\subsection{High-Dimensional Check}
\label{app:highdim}

The theory reduces a $p$-dimensional problem to the scalar margin $z$. To test which of
its claims survive that reduction, we simulate the full $p$-dimensional logistic SGD
problem directly ($p=200$, trigger on coordinate~1, clean task on the remaining
coordinates, poison setting $x_1=c$ with a flipped label), under two covariance spectra
-- flat, and power-law $\lambda_j \propto j^{-1}$ -- across $\rho\in\{0,0.2,0.4\}$,
$T\in\{2{,}000, 8{,}000\}$ and $N\in\{50,200,800\}$, 3 seeds each. This simulation uses the \emph{dense} parameterisation ($x_1 = \rho y + \sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,\xi$)
rather than the sparse model of Equation~\ref{eq:clean-x1}, because the dense form is what
admits a continuous $\rho$ sweep; it therefore tests whether the scalar reduction's
orderings survive in $p$ dimensions, not the sparse model specifically. At $q=0$ the sparse
model's clean gradient is identically zero in any dimension, so no simulation is needed
there.

\textbf{What survives.} The orderings the paper's claims rest on hold in every cell:
the attack margin increases with poison count $N$ (12/12 cells), decreases as the
trigger's overlap $\rho$ grows (12/12), and decreases with training length $T$ (15/18;
the three exceptions are $\rho=0$ power-law cells where the margin is below the seed standard deviation of about $0.07$ ($0.002$ at $T=2{,}000$ and $0.03$--$0.04$ at $T=8{,}000$)). The structure of Proposition~\ref{thm:count-regime} and
Proposition~\ref{prop:fraction-regime} is therefore not an artifact of the scalar
reduction.

\textbf{What does not.} The scalar fixed point's \emph{quantitative} predictions do not
transfer. At $\rho>0$ the measured margin is strongly negative where
Equation~\ref{eq:zstar} predicts a positive value, agreeing in sign in only $67\%$ of
cells: with the clean task training all $p$ coordinates, the trigger weight is driven
further from the attacker's target than the scalar model implies, so
Proposition~\ref{prop:fraction-regime} \emph{overestimates} attack success at $\rho>0$
in high dimensions. We therefore read Proposition~\ref{prop:fraction-regime} as consistent with the mechanism and the direction of the $\rho$ dependence, not as a quantitatively accurate threshold in the high-dimensional setting. Closing that gap
requires the random-matrix treatment used by \citet{linearpoisoning2025} and
\citet{glmbackdoor2026} for the fraction regime, which we leave open.

\subsection{Regime of Validity for Proposition~\ref{thm:count-regime}}
\label{app:validity}

\textbf{Regime of validity.} The continuum approximation is accurate when each step's
perturbation is small relative to the local curvature of $\sigma(-z)$. Simulated
against the discrete recursion under several scheduling patterns of identical total poison mass, the closed-form prediction matches sparse and irregular schedules closely and
overshoots predictably under schedules that violate the small-step condition (few,
poison-dense steps) -- a falsifiable secondary prediction: real training with very few, very
poison-dense batches should show measurably more scheduling-dependence than training
with many, sparser poisoned batches. We have not tested it; the density range our
transformer sweeps cover is confounded with placement (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}), so
we state it as an open prediction rather than a result.


\subsection{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:adam-events}}
\label{app:prop3proof}

\begin{proof}
Because $\Delta\gg1/(1-\beta_1)$, the first moment has decayed to zero before each
event. Let $u_n$ be the second moment immediately after the $n$-th event. After an
event with gradient $g$, the moments evolve as $m_{j}=\beta_1^{j}(1-\beta_1)g$ and
$v_{j}=\beta_2^{j}u_n$, so the event displaces $\theta$ by
$\eta\sum_{j\geq0}m_j/\sqrt{v_j}=\eta(1-\beta_1)g\,u_n^{-1/2}\sum_{j\ge0}(\beta_1/\sqrt{\beta_2})^{j}
=\eta(1-\beta_1)g/\bigl((1-\beta_1/\sqrt{\beta_2})\sqrt{u_n}\bigr)$.
The second moment obeys $u_n=\beta_2^{\Delta}u_{n-1}+(1-\beta_2)g^2$ with $u_0=0$,
so $u_n=(1-\beta_2)g^2(1-\beta_2^{n\Delta})/(1-\beta_2^{\Delta})$. Substituting and
summing over $n$ gives Equation~\ref{eq:adam}; $g$ cancels. The two limits follow
from $\beta_2^{\Delta}\to0$, and from $1-\beta_2^{x}\approx x(1-\beta_2)$ together
with $\sum_{n\le E}n^{-1/2}\approx2\sqrt{E}$.
\end{proof}
